A mom in a local parents group recently asked for piano lesson recommendations for her 4.5-year-old. One of the replies, from a piano teacher, advised her to wait until 5, 6, or 7 before starting private piano lessons, save her money in the meantime, and expose her child to lots of music and live concerts.

We want to be fair here, because part of that is good advice, and the part we disagree with is worth explaining rather than dismissing.

## The part that's right

If you take a 4-year-old, sit them alone at a piano bench, and run a traditional 30-minute private lesson with note reading, scales, and a practice log, it usually does not go well. Attention runs out. Frustration sets in. Parents pay for a few months and conclude their child wasn't ready.

Any teacher who has watched that happen is right to steer families away from it. And the advice to fill a young child's life with music is genuinely good. Concerts, records playing at home, singing in the car: all of it builds the ear.

But notice what actually failed in that piano lesson. It wasn't the child. It was the format. Somebody took an instructional model designed for a 10-year-old and handed it to a preschooler, and when it didn't work, the child's age took the blame.

## The part we disagree with

The advice to wait treats "music lessons" and "private piano lessons" as the same thing, and it treats everything before age 7 as passive exposure. Listening to music is wonderful. It is not the same as a child learning to keep a beat with their own hands. Change the format to match how a young child actually learns, and there's an enormous amount they can do.

Two-, three-, and four-year-olds can keep a beat. They can recognize and repeat patterns. They can hear that one chord sounds happy and another sounds sad. They can learn that music has structure, that things repeat, that something comes next and you can predict what. They can sit at a real drum kit and count to four while they play. They can find stickered notes on a piano and play a song they recognize.

None of that requires reading standard notation, sitting still for half an hour, or practicing on a schedule. It requires singing, movement, games, short bursts of hands-on instrument time, and a teacher who plans around a four-year-old's attention span instead of fighting it.

## Why waiting costs something

Here's what bothers us about "wait until 7." Those years aren't neutral. They're some of the best years a person ever gets for absorbing patterns, language, and sound. A child soaking in rhythm and pitch and structure at 3 isn't killing time until real lessons start. That is the real learning, and it's happening in the window where it comes easiest.

We see the difference on the other side. A kid who spent a year in our early classes walks into their first guitar or piano lesson already able to keep time, already recognizing notes, already hearing patterns in a song. The kid who waited starts from zero at 7, and now has to build those instincts while also learning an instrument, at an age where self-consciousness has shown up and everything feels harder to be bad at.

Public school usually gets to instruments somewhere around fourth or fifth grade. That's not a judgment of school music teachers, who do a lot with what they're given. It's a staffing and budget reality. But there's no developmental reason behind it, and it's a strange thing to treat as the natural starting line.

We've watched 4-year-olds play full songs. Parents who grew up around music tend to know this already, and usually wish someone had started them sooner.

## What we'd tell that mom

We'd tell her that her instinct was right, and that the question isn't whether her 4.5-year-old is ready. It's what kind of instruction fits a child that age.

And that's not a question we answer from a chart. We answer it by meeting the child. A consultation gives us time to hear what a parent already knows about their own kid, watch how their child responds to an instrument and to us, and figure out together what setup would serve them best.

For most kids this age, that's a small group class built for how they learn, with real instruments in their hands and games doing the heavy lifting. That's why our early childhood classes are split into [Primary Music for ages 2-4 and Kinder Music for ages 4-6](/kinder-music), each capped at four students. A 3-year-old and a 6-year-old need different rooms.

For some kids, custom private lessons are the better fit. That lets us focus on a child's specific learning style and needs. We don't use the typical 10-year-old format either. These lessons tend to bounce around on purpose: sometimes it's piano, sometimes ukulele, then maybe drums. We incorporate other instruments for an extremely valuable reason. They all work together to build an incredible musical foundation, teaching notes, scales, chords, and rhythm with hands-on experience. Sometimes we're working on songs the child picked, sometimes on timeless classics.

The ukulele is a good example of that. It's small, it's forgiving, and it's where we quietly build guitar fundamentals long before a guitar would fit in their hands. Most of that work is rhythm: learning to strum in time, to feel where the beat is and stay with it. That's the skill that separates people who can play from people who just know chords, and it's the one we work hardest to instill in teenagers and adults. A motivated 5-year-old can start absorbing it while they think they're just playing a song, and they can absolutely play real songs at that age.

That's the call we're really making when we meet a family: not whether a child is old enough, but what actually serves this particular child. Sometimes that's the group class, where kids feed off each other's energy. Sometimes it's one-on-one, where a child gets our full attention and the lesson can move at their pace. And if a child truly isn't ready yet, we'll say that too. We've told families to wait before, and we've sent families to other teachers when someone else was the better fit. What we won't do is tell a parent their preschooler can't learn music. They can, and it's a great time to start.
